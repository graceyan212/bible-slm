# Motivation & Developmental Science for Ages 7–9 (primary reference)

The evidence base for gamifying a children's Bible app **without** undermining intrinsic or faith
motivation. Two caveats up front: (a) the growth-mindset and grit literatures have real replication
debates; (b) there is **no single "Dr. Yeager for 7–9"** — Yeager's *10 to 25* is about adolescents —
but there is a strong ensemble. File 07 holds the deeper claim-by-claim citations and effect sizes.

---

## 1. Developmental psychology of motivation, 7–9

- **Erikson — Industry vs. Inferiority (~6–11):** *the competence stage.* The child's task is to master
  culturally valued skills; success yields the virtue **"competence,"** repeated failure/unfavorable
  comparison yields **inferiority**. Motivation is fed by **recognition of real accomplishment** and by
  **social comparison** (kids start measuring against peers). "I made something / I can do this" matters
  more than abstract encouragement.
- **Piaget — Concrete Operational (~7–11):** logic on *concrete, tangible* things (not abstract
  hypotheticals yet). Signature acquisitions bear directly on game mechanics:
  - **Conservation & reversibility** → cause-and-effect is reliable, so *contingent rewards* ("do X → get
    Y") are understood as fair and predictable.
  - **Classification & seriation** (sort into categories, order by dimension) → the engine behind why
    **collecting, sorting, completing sets** is intensely satisfying at this age.
  - **Decline of egocentrism** → rule-based cooperative play becomes possible.
  - **Rules become real and semi-sacred** (Piaget's *Moral Judgment of the Child*) — strong appetite for
    fairness and equal treatment.
- **Social comparison & self-concept:** around **7–8** children shift from rosy self-views to
  *comparative, evaluative* self-appraisal (Harter; Ruble). **Design implication: leaderboards/ranking
  start to "bite" psychologically at exactly this age** — motivating the top kid, demoralizing the rest.

**Net:** 7–9s are wired to **master concrete skills, follow/trust clear rules, and collect/sort/
complete.** Lean into competence + collection; be very cautious with peer-comparison mechanics.

## 2. Intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation & the overjustification effect

- **Overjustification (Lepper, Greene & Nisbett, 1973):** preschoolers who liked drawing and drew for an
  *expected* "Good Player" award later spent **less** free time drawing than kids who got no reward or an
  *unexpected* one. Expecting a reward for already-fun activity reframes play as work and interest drops.
  *This is the foundational warning against gamifying something children already enjoy.*
- **Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999) meta-analysis (128 studies):**
  - **Tangible, expected, task-contingent rewards reliably undermine intrinsic motivation — strongest for
    children** vs. college students.
  - **Verbal praise / positive feedback *enhances* intrinsic motivation** (it signals competence).
  - **Unexpected** and **non-task-contingent** rewards do little harm.
- **Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan):** intrinsic motivation is sustained when three needs are
  met — **autonomy** (I chose this), **competence** (I'm getting good), **relatedness** (I'm connected).
  Every reward has two faces: an **informational** aspect (signals competence → helps) and a
  **controlling** aspect (pressures behavior → hurts). *Make rewards feel informational, not controlling.*

**When rewards HELP vs. HURT:**
| HELP when… | HURT when… |
|---|---|
| task is boring on its own | the child **already enjoys** it |
| verbal/informational praise about effort & competence | tangible and the child works "for the prize" |
| **unexpected** ("surprise!") | **expected and announced in advance** |
| reward quality/mastery, or unrelated to completion | engagement-contingent (just for showing up/clicking) |

**Guardrails for badges/points/streaks with young kids:** (1) never gate the core enjoyable activity
behind an announced reward — reward the *side quest*, not the story; (2) prefer **unexpected, occasional**
recognition; (3) make rewards **informational** (tie to demonstrated competence/effort); (4) **fade**
rewards as intrinsic interest grows; (5) keep rewards **low-stakes and non-comparative**.

## 3. Growth mindset & process praise (Dweck), applied to 7–9

- **Mueller & Dweck (1998)** (5th-graders, ~10–11): after success, kids praised for **intelligence**
  ("you're so smart") vs. **effort** ("you worked hard") diverged sharply after a rigged failure. The
  *intelligence*-praised chose easier tasks, showed less persistence/enjoyment, and performance
  *declined*; **effort**-praised chose challenge, persisted, improved. **Praising ability backfires;
  praising process builds resilience.**
- **Process praise = strategies, effort, focus, persistence, choices** — not the person. Warn against
  *empty* effort praise ("great try!") when the child didn't try or didn't learn — praise must be
  **specific and truthful**.
- **Replication caveat (report honestly):** growth-mindset *interventions* produce **small average
  effects**, concentrated in lower-achieving/disadvantaged students (Yeager & Dweck 2019 *Nature*; Sisk
  et al. 2018). *Framing feedback with process praise is cheap and low-risk; don't oversell mindset as a
  silver bullet.*

**Feedback copy (do / don't):**
- ✅ "You kept going even when it was tricky." / "You figured out a new way to remember that story." /
  "You practiced and it's getting easier."
- ❌ "You're so smart!" / "You're a natural!" / "Genius!"
- **For faith content especially:** praise the *engagement and wondering*, not "rightness" — "You really
  thought hard about that question," **never** "Correct! +10 points."

## 4. Gamification for young children — what to use, what to avoid

- **Streaks & "streak anxiety":** exploit **loss aversion** (breaking a streak hurts more than extending
  it helps). For children (still developing emotional regulation) a broken streak can cause
  disproportionate distress/guilt/compulsion. Flagged as a **persuasive/manipulative pattern** for kids.
  If used at all: **forgiving** (freezes/grace days), short-horizon, **never punitive**; better, replace
  with a calm "continue your journey" invitation. (Cumulative/tally framing beats all-or-nothing.)
- **Variable/intermittent rewards:** the most powerful reinforcement schedule — and the mechanism behind
  slot machines/compulsive use. **Use predictable, calm rewards; avoid gambling-style unpredictability.**
- **Collection mechanics (badges/stickers/sets):** the **best-fit, lowest-risk** mechanic — rides the
  concrete-operational drive to classify/sort/complete, and is usually *achievement-based* (not announced
  task-payment), sidestepping overjustification. Keep collections about *the child's own journey*, not
  scarcity or trading pressure.
- **Points & leaderboards:** social comparison sharpens at 7–8 → competitive leaderboards help the few and
  demoralize the many. **Prefer self-referential progress** ("you vs. last week").
- **Progress bars / goal-gradient:** well-supported and benign (people accelerate near a goal). Completion
  bars/maps give competence signals without extrinsic prizes. Strong choice.
- **Narrative / quest framing:** strongly recommended — concrete-operational + mythic-literal children are
  *built for narrative*. Likely your **single best engagement engine.**
- **Avatars / customization:** support autonomy + identity/ownership; low-risk. Keep expressive, not
  pay-to-win or comparison-driven.
- **"Chocolate-covered broccoli" warning:** rewards *unrelated* to the content don't build durable
  interest — game elements must be **integrated with** the learning/faith goal.

**Dark patterns & age-appropriate design (a compliance issue, not just ethics):**
- **UK Age Appropriate Design Code / "Children's Code" (ICO), enforceable since Sept 2021** — services
  likely accessed by children should **not use nudge techniques to keep them engaged**; high-privacy
  defaults; child's best interests.
- **California Age-Appropriate Design Code Act (AB 2273, 2022)** — best-interest design + DPIAs (portions
  in First-Amendment litigation; status evolving).
- **AAP** (ages 6+): consistent limits, quality content, media shouldn't displace sleep/activity/family;
  Family Media Plan.
- **5Rights Foundation** catalogs persuasive-design harms (streaks, autoplay, variable rewards, FOMO).
- **FOMO/notifications:** avoid guilt-based re-engagement ("your friends are ahead!") — doubly
  inappropriate for a faith app; keep nudges gentle, parent-controlled, non-manipulative.

## 5. The "Dr. Yeager equivalents" for 7–9 — ranked (see file 07 for detail)

1. **Deci & Ryan (Self-Determination Theory)** — most foundational + directly applicable; the backbone
   of ethical reward design for children.
2. **Jacquelynne Eccles & Allan Wigfield (Expectancy-Value Theory)** — the leading *developmental*
   account of how motivation/task-values form across childhood; competence beliefs and values **peak in
   early elementary and decline from ~age 7**, making it directly on-point. *The closest true analog.*
3. **Susan Harter (perceived competence / self-worth)** — instrument (SPPC) validated for **ages 8–12**;
   directly on-target for self-concept and comparison vulnerability.
4. **Carol Dweck (growth mindset / process praise)** — applies to feedback/copy, with the honest caveat
   about modest intervention effects.
5. **Mark Lepper (overjustification)** — the specific researcher on *why rewarding fun backfires in kids*.
6. **Daniel Willingham (cognition of interest)** — **"stories are psychologically privileged" in
   memory**; children find problem-solving pleasurable at the right difficulty. Supports story-first,
   right-level-challenge design.
7. **Alfie Kohn** — commentator/critic (*Punished by Rewards*), not a primary researcher; aligns with
   Lepper/Deci but overstates; use as a critical lens.
8. **Angela Duckworth (grit)** — **weak fit for 7–9** (grit research centers on adolescents/adults; by
   critics' evidence *and Duckworth's own admission*). Do **not** anchor the design on grit.
9. **Jean Twenge (iGen)** — about teen/screen-time trends, **off-target for 7–9**.
10. **Dr. Becky Kennedy (Good Inside)** — practitioner, SDT-aligned; great for **parent-facing tone**
    (connection over consequences), not primary evidence.

**Evidentiary center of gravity for 7–9:** Deci & Ryan (SDT) + Lepper (overjustification) + Dweck
(process praise) + Eccles/Wigfield & Harter (developmental motivation) + Willingham (story & desirable
difficulty).

## 6. Faith-formation & moral reasoning at 7–9 (see file 07 & 09-detail)

- **Fowler — Stage 2 "Mythic-Literal Faith" (~6/7–11):** faith carried through **story/drama**, not
  abstract doctrine; **symbols/stories taken literally** (little allegory yet); strong belief in
  **reciprocity / cosmic fairness** ("just world"); God imagined as a fair anthropomorphic ruler.
  *Tell Bible stories as concrete narrative the child can enter — not abstract theology.* And **be careful
  not to reinforce a transactional "be good → get reward" theology through the reward system.**
- **Kohlberg — Preconventional (Level 1):** Stage 1 obedience/punishment (avoid punishment); Stage 2
  instrumental exchange ("what's in it for me?"), transitioning toward "good boy/girl" approval-seeking
  later. **Gilligan's critique** — Kohlberg's justice-only ladder undervalues an **ethic of care/
  relationship** (and was normed on boys). *Present morality as both fairness AND care.*
- **Berryman's Godly Play & Rebecca Nye:** children make meaning best through open **"I wonder…"
  questions** and unhurried, **non-directive** reflection — *not* right-answer quizzing. Nye's
  **S.P.I.R.I.T.** (Space, Process, Imagination, Relationship, Intimacy, Trust) is the opposite of point-
  scoring. **Strongly supports the product's retell-not-quote + wondering-to-parent direction.**
- **The "sacred vs. earned" problem:** overjustification is *especially* concerning for faith content —
  attaching points/badges to prayer, wonder, or moral reflection risks teaching that the *value is the
  prize*, crowding out intrinsic/relational meaning and reinforcing transactional theology. **Do not
  gamify the sacred act itself.** Reward *showing up, exploring, completing the journey structure*; keep
  wondering, prayer, and moral reflection **reward-free and open-ended.**

## 7. Actionable design principles (the 7 rules)

1. **Story first, rewards second.** Make narrative/quest the primary engine (Fowler + Willingham). A
   compelling story needs far fewer extrinsic hooks — and avoids the overjustification trap.
2. **Reward the journey, never the sacred act.** Gamify structure/exploration (stories explored,
   retold, places visited); keep prayer, wondering, and moral reflection reward-free.
3. **Prefer collections and progress bars over points, streaks, and leaderboards.** Collecting fits
   concrete-operational cognition and is low-risk; progress bars give competence signals without
   cash-register payouts. **Avoid child-vs-child leaderboards.**
4. **Make every reward informational, not controlling — prefer surprise over promise.** Tie recognition
   to competence/effort; deliver some rewards unexpectedly; never "do this and you'll get X" for content
   the child already enjoys.
5. **Write all feedback as truthful process praise.** "You kept going when it was tricky," never "You're
   so smart" or "Correct! +10."
6. **If you use streaks at all, make them forgiving and never punitive** (grace days, short horizons, no
   guilt notifications) — better, replace streak pressure with a calm "continue your journey" invitation.
7. **Design to the age-appropriate codes and keep the parent in the loop.** No variable/gambling reward
   loops, no FOMO pushes, high-privacy defaults; parent-facing tone modeled on connection-over-
   consequences; route "wondering" to the parent rather than auto-answering.
