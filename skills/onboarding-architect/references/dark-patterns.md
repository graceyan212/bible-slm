# Dark-pattern catalog

Stance for this whole document: **neutral menu with warnings.** Every lever below is a real, observed tactic that moves short-term conversion. This doc does not tell you which ones to use — it labels the trust/platform/legal risk on each one so the call is informed, not blind. Never refuse to discuss a pattern; always attach its risk.

How to read the catalog: each entry is a tactic, a one-line mechanism for *why* it converts, the risk category it exposes you to, and a severity rating (low/med/high) for how badly it can bite if it backfires (rejection, chargebacks, refund waves, brand damage, regulatory action).

## Catalog

| Pattern | What it is | Why it converts short-term | Risk | Severity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fake urgency / countdown timers** | A "limited time," "offer expires in 10:00" timer on the paywall or an offer screen, often on a loop that resets. | Scarcity + loss-aversion pushes users to decide now instead of comparison-shopping or bailing. | Trust — users increasingly recognize the pattern and close the paywall *expecting* a fresh countdown or better offer behind it, training them to distrust your first price. | Med |
| **Spin-the-wheel / fake "you won a discount"** | A gamified roulette/wheel shown on exit-intent; the first spin is rigged to land on a weak discount, a later spin (or a second close) lands on the "real" one. | Gamification + variable reward (slot-machine psychology) plus the illusion of having "earned" the discount rather than been given it. | Trust — outcome is predetermined/rigged, and it's a well-known format users increasingly clock as manipulative; also erodes price integrity (nobody pays list price). | High |
| **Free-trial TOGGLE paywalls** | A toggle/switch to "enable free trial," styled to look like an on/off setting rather than a purchase decision, often defaulted ON so users don't notice they're starting a paid subscription. | Ambiguates the moment of commitment; users toggle a "feature" without registering it as agreeing to be charged. | App Store — **Apple explicitly rejects this under guideline 3.1.2** as of 2026; submissions using trial toggles get bounced in review (multiple real 2026 rejections logged, e.g. "rejected again due to the free-trial toggle on the paywall"). Also stacks FTC dark-pattern risk if it ships anywhere. | High |
| **Confirmshaming opt-outs** | The decline/close button is worded to shame the user out of leaving — "No, I don't want to save money," "I don't want it," "No thanks, I'll pay full price." | Guilt/social-pressure friction on the exit path measurably reduces bounce vs. a neutral "No thanks." | Trust — users feel manipulated the moment they notice the copy is engineered against them; reviews and word-of-mouth erode. | Med |
| **Hard-to-cancel / asymmetric cancellation** | Subscribing takes one tap; canceling requires navigating a multi-screen flow, a retention survey, a "hold to cancel for 15–19 seconds" gesture, or burying the cancel path off-app (ClassPass's infamous ~17-screen flow is the canonical example). | Friction on the exit path suppresses churn purely through effort/attrition, not through actually retaining a satisfied user. | Trust + legal — FTC's "click-to-cancel" enforcement priority targets exactly this asymmetry (easy to sign up, hard to cancel); also a chargeback/refund-request magnet. | High |
| **Hidden cheap plan / pre-selected priciest option / misleading "Most Popular" badge** | The lowest-price plan is tucked behind a "view all plans" link; the default-selected radio is the most expensive tier; a "MOST POPULAR" or "Best Value" badge is placed on whichever plan the business wants sold, regardless of actual popularity. | Anchoring + default bias — most users accept whatever is pre-selected and read badges as social proof rather than persuasion copy. | Trust — badge is often simply untrue, and users who later compare plans elsewhere feel deceived; some regulators treat unsubstantiated "most popular" claims as deceptive advertising. | High |
| **Weekly pricing designed to obscure the total charge** | Framing a subscription as "$9.99/wk" instead of the ~$520/yr it compounds to, paired with no renewal-reminder notifications and copy that targets insecurity ("Am I attractive?", "Are you good enough?") to drive impulse conversion before the user does the math. | Small per-period number feels trivial; insecurity-framed hooks convert on emotion faster than rational evaluation; silence on renewal means fewer people cancel before the next charge. | Trust + legal — this is the exact playbook @athcanft documents ("$9.99/wk... NO FUCKING TRIALS," "how do you sleep at night charging so much for your app?" as a genuine incoming user complaint); chargeback and refund-request rates run high, and "renews automatically, no notification" is a live FTC dark-patterns target. | High |
| **Selling to / upselling a child in-character** | The app's mascot, persona, or in-app character directly solicits purchases from the child user rather than routing the purchase decision to the parent/payer. | Kids don't have adult-grade skepticism toward a beloved character asking them to buy something, so in-character asks convert disproportionately well versus a neutral adult-facing paywall. | Legal — squarely inside FTC/COPPA territory: collecting data from or monetizing a child without verifiable parental consent is a statutory violation, not just a UX judgment call. Epic Games paid **$275M** for COPPA violations tied to default settings and enabling in-experience purchases without parental consent; this is the highest-severity item in the catalog because it's the one most likely to trigger direct regulatory action rather than just user backlash. | High |

## The escalating-discount tension

The single most-loved lever in the corpus of app-teardown breakdowns is the **escalating exit-offer chain**: user closes the paywall → gets a "limited time" 50% off → closes *that* → gets "another" 50% off (or the classic close → 50% → close again → 80% off, or close → unlock an 80% lifetime discount). It shows up over and over across high-revenue apps ($100k/mo+) in the breakdown corpus, almost always alongside a hard paywall and a polished, mascot-driven onboarding.

**The case for it:** it is a genuinely high-converting structure. It captures the users who would otherwise bounce at list price, it lets the business hold a high anchor price for users willing to pay it, and it's cheap to implement (RevenueCat/Superwall-style exit-offer chaining is a standard, well-documented feature). Multiple breakdowns show it paired with otherwise well-regarded, polished onboarding — it's not exclusively a "sleazy app" signal.

**The case against it:** this is the *exact* mechanism that the skeptical voices in this same space call out. The Mobbin paywall study's own skeptic conclusion is to avoid aggressive discount-escalation and prefer a longer trial instead, specifically to preserve trust and LTV — discounting (their view) should be reserved for real calendar events like Black Friday, not manufactured on every exit. Apple's 2026 enforcement wave (banning "showing offers after the main paywall is closed" alongside trial toggles) puts the escalating-exit-offer *pattern itself* at rejection risk, not just the toggle variant. And the ethics-lens voice in this corpus (Filip) is on record that aggressive hard-paywalls with escalating discounts visibly correlate with *lower* ASO/product performance and higher perceived-churn — his direct claim: "I think hard paywalls hurt ASO (and product metrics)... a % of people churn just by seeing a paywall." Once a user has closed two "final" offers and seen a third appear, the "limited time" framing stops being credible, and that erosion of trust in your pricing carries into every future price move you make with that user (and, via reviews, users who never saw the flow at all).

**Verdict:** high-conversion, high-risk. Both effects are real and both are documented in the same corpus, sometimes in the same account. This document does not resolve the tension for you — it hands you the lever labeled with both sides so the call is explicit and yours: escalating discounts will lift near-term paywall conversion, and they will also cost you some trust, some ASO/organic signal, and (post-2026) some App Store review risk. Decide with eyes open, and if you use it, keep the ceiling low (e.g., stop at one exit offer, not a chain) to capture most of the upside with less of the downside.

## Platform/legal constraints

These aren't brand preferences — they're external constraints that make certain patterns costly regardless of what you'd otherwise choose. Treat every pattern in the catalog above through this filter before shipping it.

- **Apple App Store Review Guideline 3.1.2 (In-App Purchase) — no free-trial toggles, no "second paywall after close."** As of 2026, Apple is actively rejecting submissions that use a toggle/switch UI to start a free trial (it disguises the moment of financial commitment) and rejecting the "close the paywall to unlock a discount" exit-offer pattern outright. Real 2026 rejection logs in this corpus confirm both are enforced, not theoretical. If you're shipping on iOS, this converts "manipulative but effective" into "manipulative and non-shippable" — a hard gate, not a soft one.
- **COPPA (Children's Online Privacy Protection Act).** Applies to any app directed at or knowingly used by children under 13: requires verifiable parental consent before collecting personal information from a child, restricts behavioral advertising/monetization aimed at kids, and applies regardless of whether the child or the parent is the one who technically taps "buy." Enforcement is real and expensive — Epic Games' $275M FTC settlement (default privacy-invasive settings + enabling purchases without parental consent) is the benchmark case. Any pattern that solicits payment, collects data, or personalizes ads through a child-facing persona needs a parent-gate and consent flow, full stop.
- **FTC dark-patterns enforcement (2022 staff report + ongoing "click-to-cancel" rule).** The FTC has named and is actively pursuing exactly the patterns in this catalog: hard-to-cancel subscriptions (asymmetric friction between sign-up and cancellation), confirmshaming, hidden recurring charges, and manipulated default choices. The direction of travel is toward *more* enforcement, not less — a pattern that's merely "aggressive but legal" today is the kind of thing that shows up in the next staff report.

Net: platform policy and consumer-protection law are moving in the same direction — toward transparency, easy cancellation, and honest commitment moments. Patterns that rely on ambiguity, asymmetric friction, or targeting minors carry real regulatory and distribution risk on top of the trust cost.
